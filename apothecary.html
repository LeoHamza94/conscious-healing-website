The Herbs' Energetics — Hot, Cold, Dry, Moist

Once the body's terrain is understood, the plants that act on it are read through the same four qualities across nearly every tradition.

Hot vs. Cold — Hot describes stimulation, inflammation, overactivity: flushed skin, fast pulse, thirst, irritability, fever. Cold describes sluggishness and underactivity: pale skin, slow digestion, low energy, poor circulation. A hot condition calls for cooling herbs; a cold condition calls for warming ones.

Dry vs. Moist — Dry describes a lack of fluid and lubrication: cracked skin, dry cough, constipation, brittle hair and nails. Moist (or damp) describes excess fluid and congestion: swelling, mucus, water retention, heaviness. A dry condition calls for moistening, demulcent herbs; a damp condition calls for drying, astringent ones.

These two axes combine into four broad patterns, and matching a plant's energetics to a body's — rather than a disease name — is the throughline connecting every tradition on this page: the tree above, the chakra system below, and the herb library still to come.

One Body, Three Instruments

The Three Circuits

Every framework on this page — the doshas, the five elements, Western tissue states, the chakras and ganglia, the dhatus and the five substances — is really a description of the body along one of three circuits: biochemical, energetic, and somatic. None of the three is more real than the others; they're three different instruments reading the same body.

Biochemical Circuit

The Physical Terrain

Focus: Cellular communication, electrical charge, alkaline balance.

Question: What is the body made of, and does it have what it needs?

Frameworks: Yahki Hickman's framework, mineral terrain.

Energetic Circuit

The Vital Force

Focus: Circulation of vital force, movement, blockages.

Question: How is energy moving, and where is it blocked or depleted?

Frameworks: Chakras (Ganglia Clusters), Nadis, Doshas, Five Elements, Prana, Qi.

Somatic Circuit

The Tissue Structure

Focus: Physical tissue states, structural tension, laxity, heat, cold.

Question: What is the tissue itself physically doing right now?

Frameworks: Matthew Wood's Western Physiomedical States, Ayurvedic Dhatus.

A single imbalance often shows up in all three circuits at once, described in three different languages: a depleted mineral terrain (biochemical) may show as stagnant Qi (energetic) and as cold, atrophied tissue (somatic) — three readings of one underlying pattern. Plant medicine works because herbs can meet a person in whichever circuit is speaking loudest, at whichever entry point makes sense to them. This is the whole premise beneath Conscious-Healing: there is no single correct language for the body, only the one that helps a particular person understand themselves.

Traditional African Medicine (TAM)

The Tripartite Theory & the Vital Force

One of humanity's oldest continuous medical traditions, and a system still central to healthcare for hundreds of millions across the African continent today.

Illness is read through three possible realms of imbalance: the natural/physical, the social/environmental, and the spiritual/ancestral — each requiring its own path back to harmony. Underlying all three is a vital life-force energy, known in Yoruba cosmology as Ashe and related to the concept of Seriti in Sotho and Tswana tradition — health is a strong, radiant vital force; illness is what takes hold when that force dims.

Treatment traditionally proceeds on two fronts at once: physical cleansing through bitter herbal decoctions, and spiritual or social cleansing through community ritual and restoration — healing the body and the relationships around it together.

Ayurvedic Medicine

The Three Doshas

Vata, Pitta, Kapha — combinations of the elements that govern how a body tends to move, metabolize, and hold structure.

Vata (air + ether) governs movement — breath, circulation, nerve impulses. In excess: dryness, anxiety, irregular digestion, cold extremities. Balanced by warmth, routine, and grounding.

Pitta (fire + water) governs transformation — digestion, metabolism, body temperature. In excess: heat, inflammation, acid reflux, irritability. Balanced by cooling, moderation, and unstructured time.

Kapha (earth + water) governs structure — tissue, lubrication, immunity. In excess: congestion, weight gain, lethargy, stagnancy. Balanced by stimulation, movement, and lightness.

Most people carry a blend of two doshas with one dominant, and that pattern shifts seasonally, with stress, and with age.

Traditional Chinese Medicine

The Five Elements

Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water — five elements, each governing an organ system, an emotion, and a season, mapped through the cycles that generate or restrain one another.

Wood (Liver/Gallbladder) governs planning and the smooth flow of energy. Season: Spring. Blocked, it shows as frustration and rigidity.

Fire (Heart/Small Intestine) governs joy and circulation. Season: Summer. Excessive, it shows as agitation and insomnia.

Earth (Spleen/Stomach) governs nourishment and grounding. Season: Late Summer. Destabilized, it shows as worry and poor digestion.

Metal (Lung/Large Intestine) governs structure and letting go. Season: Autumn. Unresolved, it shows as grief and rigidity.

Water (Kidney/Bladder) governs reserve and deep vitality. Season: Winter. Depleted, it shows as fear and exhaustion.

The elements move through two cycles: generating (Wood feeds Fire, Fire creates Earth, Earth bears Metal, Metal collects Water, Water nourishes Wood) and restraining (Wood parts Earth, Earth dams Water, Water douses Fire, Fire melts Metal, Metal chops Wood) — no organ system exists in isolation.

Western Physiomedical States

Tension, laxity, heat, cold, damp, dry — how tissue itself is behaving, read as a practical guide toward which plants may be indicated.

This system reads the tissue itself, in oppositional pairs: Tension vs. Laxity (too tight and contracted vs. too loose and unsupported), Heat vs. Cold (running hot and inflamed vs. sluggish and underactive), Damp vs. Dry (holding excess moisture vs. lacking it), and the secondary states Atrophy vs. Excitation (wasting and underfed vs. overstimulated and irritated).

The practical use is matching — a hot, dry, atrophied tissue calls for cooling, moistening, nourishing herbs, never the reverse. This system is prized for reading what's happening right now in the tissue, rather than assigning a fixed lifelong type.

Biochemical & Alkaline Framework

Understanding the body as an electrical system requiring the right terrain to heal.

This framework reads the body's terrain — its biochemical and electrical environment — rather than a constitutional type or tissue behavior. The body is understood as an electrical system: nerve function, muscle contraction, and cellular communication all depend on charge, which depends on mineral balance, particularly alkaline minerals like potassium, calcium, and magnesium.

Terrain over pathogen: rather than asking what's attacking the body, this framework asks what environment allowed dysfunction to take hold. Diet and mineral-rich herbs are used to shift the body's internal environment, rather than targeting a symptom directly.

This is the deepest layer for a reason — it's the soil the rest of the tree grows from, before energetics, tissue states, or elemental patterns come into play.

The Seven Dhatus

Ayurveda describes the body's tissues through seven dhatus, built in sequence, each nourishing the next:

  • Rasa (plasma/lymph) — nourishment and hydration, the first tissue formed from digested food.
  • Rakta (blood) — oxygenation and vitality.
  • Mamsa (muscle) — physical strength and protection of vital organs.
  • Meda (fat) — lubrication and insulation.
  • Asthi (bone) — structure and support.
  • Majja (bone marrow and nerve tissue) — filling of bone cavities, transmission of sensation.
  • Shukra (reproductive tissue) — the final and most refined tissue, governing reproduction and vitality.

Each dhatu depends on the one before it being properly nourished — poor digestion weakens rasa, which weakens every tissue built afterward. Imbalance in a dhatu shows up as either excess (ama, or toxic buildup) or depletion (kshaya), and herbs are chosen to correct whichever direction the tissue has drifted.

The Five Substances

TCM organizes the body's tissue and vital function through five substances, sometimes called the Five Treasures:

  • Qi — the vital energy animating all bodily function; its movement, or stagnation, underlies nearly every pattern of disharmony.
  • Blood (Xue) — nourishes and moistens tissue, closely tied to Qi (Qi moves Blood, Blood houses Qi).
  • Body Fluids (Jin-Ye) — all fluids apart from blood: saliva, sweat, tears, mucus, synovial fluid — governing moisture and lubrication throughout the body.
  • Jing (Essence) — the deepest reserve, inherited at birth and slowly spent across a lifetime, governing growth, reproduction, and aging.
  • Shen (Spirit) — consciousness, mental clarity, and the animating presence behind the eyes; disturbed Shen shows as insomnia, anxiety, or a scattered mind.

Where Ayurveda builds tissue in sequence (dhatu to dhatu), TCM reads tissue through the sufficiency and free movement of these five substances — stagnant Qi, deficient Blood, or depleted Jing each point toward a different category of herb.

Ganglia Clusters — The Chakras, Read Through the Nervous System

Chakra means wheel, energy center, vortex. Observed from the physical body, chakras are ganglia — clusters of neurons that form part of the peripheral nervous system, carrying electrical signals to and from the central nervous system. Biochemist Yahki Hickman teaches that the chakras correspond directly to the endocrine system: clusters of glands secreting hormones throughout the body. Where Ayurveda calls it prana moving through energy centers, biochemistry calls it electrical and hormonal signaling through neural and glandular clusters — two languages describing the same architecture.

This isn't only a modern reframing. Research such as Rajesh Kumar Manik's neuro-anatomical study of the shadachakra (six-chakra system) has examined the anatomical and physiological grounding of the chakra system directly, connecting it to established nervous system structures. And the crossover itself — left hemisphere logical, right hemisphere artistic, each controlling the opposite side of the body — produces the same double-helix pattern that ascends and descends the spinal cord, the same crossing structure reflected in the chakra system's channels.

Those channels are the Nadis — most centrally, Ida, Pingala, and Sushumna — the three primary pathways connecting the seven chakras, moving prana the way the circulatory system moves blood. When energy stagnates or grows atrophied in these channels, Ayurvedic teaching holds that the imbalance eventually manifests as physical symptom — the layer biomedicine tends to treat only after it surfaces in the body, rather than at the energetic root.

Sajah Popham teaches that the seven chakras are the primary "organs" of the astral body — the energetic body that carries imbalance before a physical ailment ever appears. Each chakra also maps to specific physical organs and systems:

  • Root — testes, kidneys, spine.
  • Sacral — bladder, prostate, ovaries, kidneys, gallbladder, bowel, spleen.
  • Solar Plexus — intestines, pancreas, liver, bladder, stomach, upper spine.
  • Heart — heart, lungs.
  • Throat — bronchial tubes, vocal cords, respiratory system, tongue, esophagus.
  • Third Eye — eyes, pituitary gland, pineal gland, brain.
  • Crown — spinal cord, brain stem, the central nervous system as a whole.

This is the layer where East and West genuinely converge rather than compete: Matthew Wood's Western constitutional energetics — heat/excitation, damp/stagnation, wind/tension, dry/atrophy, cold/depression — describe the same underlying stagnation-or-atrophy dynamic that Ayurveda names through the tridoshas (vata, pitta, kapha) and their disruption. Different vocabulary, same body.
